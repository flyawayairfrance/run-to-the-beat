The science, in plain wordsOnly findings from peer-reviewed studies, with the source under each one.

Music makes running feel better

g = 0.48

The biggest and most reliable effect. Across 139 studies and 3,599 people, music clearly improved how good exercise felt (affective valence).

Terry et al. 2020, meta-analysis, Psychological Bulletin

A real but modest performance boost

g = 0.31

Music was linked to better physical performance, and the effect was bigger with fast music than with slow-to-medium music.

Terry et al. 2020

Effort feels a bit lighter

g = 0.22

Perceived exertion went down a little, and oxygen use was slightly more efficient (g = 0.15). Heart rate did not change significantly.

Terry et al. 2020

Running on the beat helps most

+18–20%

Elite triathletes running in time with music lasted 18.1% (motivational music) and 19.7% (neutral music) longer to exhaustion than with no music, and ran more economically.

Terry et al. 2012, J Sci Med Sport

It's the beat more than the song

A plain metronome matched to cadence extended time to exhaustion as much as motivational music did. A strong, steady beat helps you hold pace; music you love adds the mood lift.

Bood et al. 2013, PLoS One · Terry et al. 2012

Tempo can quietly shift your cadence

Speeding music up or slowing it down by 1–3%, too little to notice, pulled runners' step rate with it. Runners synced much more when they actually tried to match the beat.

Van Dyck et al. 2015, Sports Med Open · Van Dyck et al. 2021, Ann NY Acad Sci

Faster tempo as you push harder

As exercise got harder, people preferred fast music (about 135–140 BPM) over medium. Slow music (95–100 BPM) wasn't preferred at any intensity.

Karageorghis et al. 2011, Res Q Exerc Sport

Its limit: the very hard stuff

Music works best in self-paced, steady efforts. Above the anaerobic threshold (all-out intervals), it no longer seems to make the effort feel easier, though it can still lift mood and arousal.

Karageorghis & Priest 2012, review Parts I and II

Keep one ear open

A US case series found 116 reports of pedestrians injured or killed while wearing headphones, mostly in cities and often near trains or traffic. In Bangkok traffic, use one earbud or open-ear headphones and keep the volume down.

Lichenstein et al. 2012, Injury Prevention

Tempo guide by type of runThere are two ways to use tempo: sync your steps to the beat, or just play it in the background. Pick a run type.

First, find your cadence (takes 30 seconds)

Run at the effort you want, count your steps for 30 seconds, and multiply by 2. That's your steps per minute. A song works for syncing when its BPM equals your cadence, or half of it (one step on every beat versus one step per half-beat).

Song BPMs from songbpm.com, checked 16 Oct 2026. Rock and pop only. "85 → 170" means you take one step per half-beat. The background bands come from Karageorghis et al. 2011; the per-run advice is my synthesis of the studies above, not a lab-tested prescription for each run type.

How to make it a fair test

Alternate music and no-music runs on the same route and type of run, at a similar time of day, so heat and fatigue don't fake a result. Aim for at least 4 of each per run type before trusting the comparison. Judge mainly by how hard it felt (RPE) and how good it felt, since that's where the research shows the clearest effect; pace will move less.
